\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this report, we designed a multi-agent system of six crews and thirteen agents to detect
and solve air traffic control conflicts in a static scenario. On top of the four mandatory
crews, we added two more: Airspace Constraints and Safety Validation. The first one turns
the weather, the restricted zones and the airport constraints into constraint zones, and the
second one independently verifies every proposed manoeuvre. These decisions follow from the
environment analysis and the five design rules we agreed on. Since the scenario is static,
accessible, deterministic and discrete, every calculation can be done by deterministic
tools. However, it is non-episodic within a single run, so we needed a validation loop to
prevent a solution from creating new conflicts.

The main idea behind our architecture has been separating the numerical calculations from
the decision-making. In a safety-critical environment like air traffic control, we could not
allow the language model to calculate distances or control the flow of the system. That is
why we wrapped the course detector in a single shared deterministic tool and left the
routing and the retry limit in the hands of the CrewAI Flow. In this way, most of the agents
act in a reactive and predictable way, and reasoning is reserved for the agents that really
need it: EC is deliberative, and CA, SUP and RO are hybrid. As a safeguard, a conflict that
is still rejected after the third attempt is flagged for a human controller. Overall, the system
works as a cooperative MAS with a hybrid structure that combines information, collaborative
and interface agents, without needing a facilitator agent, since the CrewAI Flow handles
the coordination.

Another advantage of this design is its modularity. By unifying weather, restricted zones
and airport constraints into a single constraint zone model, the detector and the Safety
Validation crew can treat every restriction in the same way. Moreover, the exchange of data
through intermediate files based on Pydantic models allows us to develop and test every crew
separately during the implementation phase.

We are aware that this model simplifies reality: real air traffic control is dynamic,
continuous and uncertain, because aircraft do not follow their plans exactly and new
flights and weather keep arriving. Once the basic system works, we will complete the second
step of the wind design, the wind-corrected ETAs. Beyond that, there are some clear
improvements for the future: dividing independent conflicts into groups to solve them in
parallel, saving past solutions with case-based reasoning, and choosing the runway according
to the wind if more detailed airport data is available.
